Long-term forecasts combine historical earthquake records, prehistoric fault evidence, and tectonic setting. Recurrence and potential event sizes help estimate hazard over an interval. Hazard is generally high near many active plate boundaries, including subduction zones and collision belts. New Madrid and Charleston illustrate that some intraplate areas also have important hazard. A hazard map describes an expected shaking measure or probability over a defined time. Read its legend and date; a historical forecast map is not a newly issued forecast.

A seismic gap is a segment with less earthquake activity than neighboring segments or than expected from its history. In the chapter example, some quiet segments were interpreted as locked and accumulating strain. Quiet does not automatically mean safe. Nor does a gap establish exactly when, where, and how large the next earthquake will be. A creeping segment and a locked segment can behave differently. Combine deformation measurements with earthquake history rather than treating a lack of dots as a complete explanation.

The Parkfield exercise used repeated events to anticipate another earthquake during a specified window; the eventual 2004 event occurred later. An average interval is not a clock. Monitoring small motions, tilt, or possible foreshocks can inform research, but there is no reliable method for predicting a specific earthquake’s exact time, location, and magnitude. An early warning detects an earthquake that has already begun and can alert places before stronger shaking arrives. It is different from predicting the event before rupture starts.
