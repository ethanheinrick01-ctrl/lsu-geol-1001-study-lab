The March 2011 Tohoku earthquake occurred on the subduction interface offshore of northeastern Japan. A large rupture displaced the seafloor and generated a destructive tsunami. The tsunami inundated coastal communities and damaged power and cooling systems at Fukushima. This links a geological event to cascading infrastructure failure. Aftershocks outline part of the affected fault region and can damage weakened structures. A tsunami forecast map shows modeled wave amplitudes, not a direct map of earthquake magnitudes.

The 2010 Haiti earthquake occurred near a complex plate-boundary fault system with strike-slip and thrust motion. Severe building collapse caused major losses; “near a transform boundary” does not prove that every fault slipped purely horizontally. The 2023 Turkey–Syria sequence occurred in a region of interacting faults and plates. Major strike-slip ruptures and numerous aftershocks affected a densely settled area. Building vulnerability, population exposure, and disrupted services help explain losses. A smaller magnitude can cause catastrophic damage when the rupture is close to vulnerable communities.

The textbook and lecture use historical estimates of magnitude, deaths, and damage. Those estimates should be tied to their source date rather than presented as a permanent leaderboard. The supplied October 27 lecture also compares New Zealand events. The current textbook section instead emphasizes Turkey–Syria; these are different source selections, not grounds to silently rename one event as another. For comparison practice, identify the tectonic setting, the immediate physical hazard, and the factors that turn that hazard into losses.
