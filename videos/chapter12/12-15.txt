Reflection sends part of the wave energy back from an interface. Refraction changes the direction of transmitted energy when the wave enters material with a different seismic velocity. For the source’s downward-traveling ray, entering faster material bends the ray toward a shallower angle to a horizontal interface. Entering slower material bends it closer to the interface normal. Wave speed depends on elastic properties and density together. A general increase in mantle velocity with depth is not a rule that density alone always makes every wave faster.

Near an earthquake, the shorter path through crust can produce the first arrival. Farther away, a path that enters faster mantle can arrive before the slower crustal path despite its greater length. The distance where the first arrival changes constrains crustal thickness when combined with velocities and a model. The Moho is the crust–mantle boundary. A reflected travel time includes the trip down and back up. For the textbook’s idealized 6 km/s crust, a 10-second vertical round trip corresponds to 30 km depth, not 60 km.

Compressional waves, called P-waves, refract strongly at the core–mantle boundary, leaving a zone without direct P arrivals. A P-wave shadow is not evidence that P-waves cannot travel through liquid. Shear waves, called S-waves, do not cross the liquid outer core. The broad S-wave shadow, together with other observations, supports a molten outer core. Reflected and converted waves can take indirect paths. Keep direct-wave shadow claims separate from the claim that no seismic energy of any kind could ever be detected there.
