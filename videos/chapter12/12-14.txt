Volcanic inclusions can bring pieces of older rocks toward the surface. Uplift and erosion can expose rocks that once formed at depth. Mines expose rocks along excavated workings. Drill cores recover cylindrical samples along boreholes. These are direct observations with limited spatial coverage, not a complete view of Earth’s interior. Surface outcrops and mine material can offer accessible evidence of subsurface rock types. Check where a sample came from before using it to reconstruct its original position.

Seismic-reflection surveys send energy into the ground and record returning signals with geophones. Reflections can reveal boundaries, folds, faults, and layered geometry. Magnetic surveys measure variations in magnetic field strength related to rock properties. Gravity surveys measure small gravitational variations related to density contrasts. Electrical surveys measure conductivity or resistivity. Water-bearing material and clay can influence electrical response; a conductive anomaly does not uniquely identify one rock type.

The buried-channel example combines low-density sediment, relatively low magnetism, and a conductivity pattern consistent with wetter material. Each measurement constrains a different property. A seismic profile is processed data; a colored geologic cross section is an interpretation based on those data and other evidence. They are not the same kind of observation. Several subsurface configurations may fit one data set. Combining samples, geology, and multiple surveys helps reduce ambiguity.
