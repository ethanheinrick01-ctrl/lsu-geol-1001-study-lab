A tsunami begins when a disturbance rapidly moves a large volume of water. Vertical seafloor displacement during megathrust rupture is a major mechanism. Before rupture, a locked interface lets the overriding plate deform. When it slips, the seafloor can change shape abruptly and displace the overlying water. Landslides entering water or moving underwater can also generate tsunamis. Volcanic explosions and collapse, as in the Krakatau example, can displace water without requiring a megathrust.

In deep water, a tsunami can travel very fast with a long wavelength and modest height. It affects a much deeper water column than an ordinary wind wave. In shallow water it slows, its wavelength shortens, and its height can increase. Coastal shape and bathymetry influence the eventual runup and flooding. A tsunami is often a series of surges and strong currents, not one picturesque breaking wave. An initial withdrawal of the sea can occur, but not every tsunami begins with visible withdrawal.

The 1960 Chile event produced a tsunami that crossed the Pacific and damaged Hawaii and Japan. A community far from the earthquake can still face a tsunami. Coastal inundation can destroy buildings, move boats inland, and carry debris. Large landslide-generated local waves can also arrive very quickly. Warning systems combine earthquake information with sea-level and ocean-bottom measurements. Detection and forecasting support warnings; they do not predict the exact earthquake before it begins.
