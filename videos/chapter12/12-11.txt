Map the location, orientation, and extent of a rupture. Measure displaced streams, roads, fences, and rock units to determine movement direction and amount. A single-event offset differs from cumulative displacement across many earthquakes. Match the same feature across the fault before calculating its displacement. Scarps, linear valleys, ridges, ponds, and diverted drainage can reveal faults. An isolated linear feature is a clue that needs corroboration, not proof by itself.

A trench across a fault exposes displaced sediment and soil layers. Older layers can preserve several events while younger layers record fewer. Dated charcoal or peat can bracket deformation. Date the relevant material and interpret its relationship to the fault; flowing stream water cannot date the last fault movement. The North Anatolian example distinguishes a 1.59 m component of 1999 scarp displacement from a larger cumulative scarp. Much motion was horizontal, so scarp height alone is not total slip.

Before-and-after satellite radar measurements can form an interferogram. Colored fringes represent measured ground displacement along the satellite’s viewing direction; they are not rock colors or a direct stress map. Laser surveying and repeated surface measurements detect deformation. Instruments in a borehole through an active fault can record conditions at depth. Combining field relations, dating, and geophysical measurements constrains history and recurrence. It does not establish the exact time of the next earthquake.
