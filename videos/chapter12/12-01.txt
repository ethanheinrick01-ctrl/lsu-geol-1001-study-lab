An earthquake is a sudden release of energy stored in rock. Seismic waves carry part of that energy outward through Earth and along its surface; the disturbance can be detected far from its source. The hypocenter, also called the focus, is the place where rupture begins. The epicenter is the point on the surface directly above it. A rupture can spread far beyond its starting point. A seismic station contains instruments that record ground motion. For comparable paths, a nearby station receives a given wave before a more distant station. Read the actual station positions rather than memorizing a station number.

A normal fault lowers the hanging wall relative to the footwall and commonly accommodates extension. A reverse fault raises the hanging wall; a gently dipping reverse fault is a thrust fault. A strike-slip fault displaces its two sides mainly horizontally. Normal, reverse, thrust, and strike-slip faults can all generate earthquakes when they slip abruptly. The fault type describes relative movement. It does not, by itself, supply the earthquake magnitude, hypocentral depth, or expected damage.

Explosive eruptions, magma pushing into rock, faulting beneath a heavy volcano, and collapse of unstable volcanic slopes can produce seismic signals. Distinctive volcanic tremors can accompany magma movement. Large landslides shake the ground as rock breaks and moves. Underground explosions compress surrounding material and generate waves that seismic networks can detect. Reservoir filling adds a load to the crust; deep wastewater injection can alter conditions on existing faults. These are human-induced mechanisms, not evidence that every earthquake near a well or reservoir has the same cause.
