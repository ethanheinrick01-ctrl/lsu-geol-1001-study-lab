New oceanic lithosphere is hot and thin. Only its relatively shallow, cool part behaves brittlely, so ridge earthquakes are generally shallow and small to moderate. Normal faulting and magma entering fissures generate earthquakes along the ridge axis. Strike-slip motion occurs on transform faults connecting adjacent ridge segments. As a plate moves away from the ridge, cooling, subsidence, and bending can activate faults away from the axis. Not every ridge-system earthquake lies on the spreading axis.

Bending before the trench can fracture the approaching plate. Thrust faults in the accretionary prism accommodate scraped-off material. The megathrust is the plate-contact thrust fault. Large slip and vertical seafloor displacement can produce destructive tsunamis; not every subduction earthquake produces one. The cold descending slab supports intermediate and deep earthquakes, approaching 700 km in the supplied chapter. This deep slab seismicity is distinct from a shallow megathrust rupture. The overriding plate can also have thrusting, magma movement, and volcanic seismicity.

Continental collision spreads deformation across a broad zone. Thrusting near the boundary and reactivation of faults inside either plate can produce earthquakes; strike-slip and normal motion can also accommodate regional deformation. Continental rifts commonly generate normal-fault earthquakes. Continental transform faults generate mostly shallow strike-slip earthquakes, sometimes large ones. Magma intrusion and renewed stress on old faults provide intraplate mechanisms. New Madrid illustrates that distance from a modern boundary does not make a continent’s interior immune.
