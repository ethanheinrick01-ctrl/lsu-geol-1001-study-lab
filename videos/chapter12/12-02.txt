Stress is force per area; strain is the resulting change in shape. A locked fault can resist motion while nearby rock bends and stores elastic strain energy. Elastic behavior means the material can return toward its former shape when the load is removed. Friction and rock strength help resist slip until the applied stress exceeds that resistance. The earthquake-cycle graph rises gradually during loading and drops abruptly during slip. The graph is an explanatory model, not a calendar for predicting the next earthquake.

Rupture starts in a small area at the hypocenter and expands along a fault surface. The entire fault plane does not begin moving simultaneously. If rupture reaches the ground surface, it can create a fault scarp or other surface break. A rupture may expand farther in one direction than the other. Rupture stops when the remaining stress cannot overcome resistance farther along the fault. An earthquake does not have to break every part of the fault.

After slip, some elastic deformation is recovered, but the displacement across the fault is permanent. An offset stream or displaced road does not return to its old alignment simply because the surrounding rock rebounds. Repeated locking and slipping is stick-slip behavior. The recurrence interval is the average time between comparable repeated events, not a guaranteed waiting period. Fresh scarps are sharp; erosion rounds their upper edges and deposits sediment at their bases. Scarp shape and dated deposits can help reconstruct earlier events.
