Ground motion can bend, shake, or collapse structures. Performance depends on design, connections, materials, and the local shaking, not simply whether a building is made of one named material. Loose sediment can amplify motion relative to firm bedrock. A structure’s natural period can also interact with the shaking, so proximity to a fault alone does not explain every damaged building. Ground rupture can offset roads and foundations. On steep slopes, shaking can trigger landslides and rock falls that threaten sites beyond the immediate fault trace.

Liquefaction occurs when shaking causes loose, water-saturated sediment to lose much of its strength. Buildings can settle or tilt as their support fails; the sediment has not melted into magma. Aftershocks follow stress changes caused by the main earthquake. They can damage structures already weakened by the first shock, even when their magnitudes are smaller. Both foundation conditions and structural condition matter after the main shock. A building that remains standing is not automatically undamaged or safe.

Broken gas lines can feed fires; broken water mains can hinder firefighting. Dam failures and land subsidence can cause flooding. Underwater displacement can generate a tsunami. Hazard maps, stronger construction, base isolation, secured utilities, and emergency planning can reduce losses. They cannot make an area completely free of earthquake risk. Current safety guidance: Drop, Cover, and Hold On under sturdy nearby furniture when possible. The supplied course material includes doorway advice; current USGS guidance rejects the doorway as the preferred refuge. Avoid open flames when gas lines may be damaged.
