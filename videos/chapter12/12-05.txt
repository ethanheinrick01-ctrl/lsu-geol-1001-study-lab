A seismic wave transfers energy through material. Individual particles oscillate near their positions; they do not travel all the way from the earthquake to a recording station. P-waves are compressional body waves. Particle motion is parallel to wave propagation, and these waves travel through solids and liquids. They arrive before S-waves along comparable paths. S-waves are shear body waves. Particle motion is perpendicular to propagation. They travel through solids but cannot propagate as shear waves through liquid.

Love waves produce horizontal side-to-side motion perpendicular to their travel direction. Rayleigh waves produce rolling, elliptical motion with vertical and horizontal components. Both are surface-wave types, distinct from P and S body waves. Surface waves can cause destructive building motion; structural response also depends on wave period and building properties. Amplitude is the size of the displacement. Period is the time for one full cycle. Resonance can intensify shaking when the forcing period matches a structure’s natural response.

A seismometer senses ground motion. In the mechanical illustration the frame moves with the ground while a suspended mass resists motion through inertia; their relative movement makes a record. Three perpendicular components record north–south, east–west, and vertical motion. A seismogram is the resulting plot of ground motion against time, not the instrument itself. In the supplied record, background vibrations precede P-wave arrival, then S-wave arrival, then larger surface-wave motion. Increasing source distance generally increases the P–S interval.
