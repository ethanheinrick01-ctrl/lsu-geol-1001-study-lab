The 1964 Alaska earthquake was a very large subduction-related event, reported as moment magnitude 9.2 in the course sources. It began beneath southern Alaska and ruptured a broad megathrust surface. The downgoing Pacific plate moved beneath the overriding North American plate. The textbook cross section shows a gently dipping interface, not a continental rift or a purely strike-slip boundary. The aftershock distribution helped constrain the large affected fault area. The felt region and the region of severe property damage were different sizes.

Shaking damaged Anchorage and triggered major landslides. Weak ground and slope failure caused buildings and neighborhoods to settle or collapse. Some coastal areas rose while others subsided. Uplift exposed marine organisms above their former tide level; subsidence allowed seawater to flood former dry land. Seafloor displacement generated a Pacific tsunami, while local submarine landslides produced additional waves. Do not assume every local wave height represents the same source mechanism.

Surveyors mapped uplift and subsidence rather than assuming a uniform elevation change. The coherent regional pattern supported thrusting at a convergent margin. This work helped establish the subduction interpretation during the development of plate tectonics. The nearby volcanic arc provides an additional regional clue to subduction. The map’s broad boundary of ground and ice cracking marks an affected region; it must not be read as one continuous fault fissure thousands of kilometers long.
