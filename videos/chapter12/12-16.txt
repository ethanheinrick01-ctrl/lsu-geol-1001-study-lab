Exhumed metamorphic rocks preserve minerals and structures formed at depth. Laboratory experiments test how rock responds to pressure and temperature. Numerical models explore possible flow and wave paths under stated physical assumptions. Models are tested with observations; they are not direct photographs of inaccessible depths. Together these approaches constrain conditions and processes that a surface exposure alone cannot reveal.

Seismic tomography combines many rays passing through a region from different directions. Earlier-than-expected arrivals can indicate faster material; later arrivals can indicate slower material. In the supplied diagram, the granite body is a faster region relative to surrounding sedimentary rocks. Rays that sample it can arrive earlier than predicted by a uniform-background model. The red and blue colors encode relative velocity in this particular figure. They are not literal rock colors, and they are not the lab’s reading-emphasis colors. Sparse ray coverage limits resolution.

Slower regions can be consistent with hotter material; faster regions can reflect cooler or stiffer material. Composition and other physical properties also matter, so velocity alone is not a unique thermometer. The chapter presents models with cool subducted slabs sinking and hot, mostly solid material rising. It discusses the D-double-prime region near the core–mantle boundary and possible links to plumes.
