The San Andreas system accommodates relative motion between the Pacific and North American plates. Its segments differ in creeping behavior, locking, and earthquake history. The northern segment includes the 1906 rupture; the Loma Prieta earthquake affected part of the broader system in 1989. Nearby faults can also produce damaging earthquakes. The central creeping segment moves gradually at the surface. Creep does not establish that every depth or adjacent fault is incapable of earthquake rupture.

Parkfield has been studied because of repeated moderate earthquakes. The southern segment associated with the 1857 Fort Tejon event is an important example of long-term locking. Historical probability statements require their original date and time window. The textbook’s rolling “next 30 years” phrase is not a current forecast simply because it appears on this page. A restraining bend in the southern system produces compression and thrusting. This helps explain why thrust-fault earthquakes can occur within a region famous for strike-slip motion.

Look for offset streams, linear valleys and ridges, scarps, ponds, and drainages aligned with the fault. Match the aerial photograph with the named features in the source illustration. Trenches and displaced rock units can reveal movement over different timescales. An abandoned stream channel can preserve an earlier alignment. The East California Shear Zone contains related active faults east of the San Andreas. Hazard assessment must include the network, not just the best-known named fault.
