Measure the interval between the first P and S arrivals on each seismogram. A larger P–S interval generally indicates a more distant source; use the supplied travel-time relation rather than inventing one universal speed. In the example, ISCO has the shortest interval, DUG is intermediate, and WUAZ has the longest. The plotted distance is a radius around a station, not a direction toward the source. Draw distance circles around at least three stations. Their shared intersection locates the epicenter in the simplified exercise. Real networks use many records and more detailed models.

Measure the maximum S-wave amplitude in the chapter’s local-magnitude exercise and use the distance inferred from the arrival interval. Connect distance and amplitude on the outer scales. The line crosses the middle magnitude scale at the estimated local magnitude. Separate station measurements should converge on one event magnitude after accounting for distance. A one-unit magnitude increase corresponds to ten times the ground-motion amplitude in this scale and approximately 32 times the energy. Two magnitude units correspond to roughly 1,000 times the energy, not 64.

Local magnitude uses an amplitude-and-distance relationship. Moment magnitude uses seismic moment, related to the area that slipped, amount of slip, and rigidity; it is useful for large earthquakes. Modified Mercalli intensity describes observed shaking and damage at a place, using Roman numerals I–XII. Different sites can have different intensities during one earthquake. Local ground conditions and building vulnerability influence damage. The site with the greatest damage is useful evidence, but damage alone does not identify the precise hypocenter or replace instrument data.
