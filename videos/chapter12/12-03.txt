Most earthquakes cluster along plate boundaries, but some occur within plates. A continental coast is not automatically a plate boundary: Atlantic passive margins differ from the active western margins of the Americas. The supplied maps use yellow for 0–70 km, green for 70–300 km, and red for depths greater than 300 km. These colors are the source map legend, not the lab’s reading-emphasis categories. Mid-ocean ridges have shallow earthquakes. Subduction zones combine shallow, intermediate, and deep earthquakes along a descending slab.

Start near the trench and follow the sequence from shallow to deeper earthquakes. That depth progression traces the inclined slab; the deepest dots do not mark the trench. Near Japan the supplied pattern deepens westward, so the Pacific slab descends toward the west. Beneath western South America the pattern deepens eastward, so the slab descends beneath the continent. A map plots epicenters at the surface and uses color to encode hypocentral depth. A cross section makes the inclined earthquake zone easier to see.

The Mediterranean-to-Asia belt includes continental collision and other convergence. Eastern Africa has a diffuse rift-related seismic zone. Low-seismicity interiors separate many active belts. The Aleutians mark subduction south of Alaska; the San Andreas marks continental transform motion. Hawaii illustrates volcanic and associated seismic activity within a plate. Shallow seismicity follows the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. Broad ocean interiors and passive margins generally have less seismicity, which does not mean zero possible earthquakes.
