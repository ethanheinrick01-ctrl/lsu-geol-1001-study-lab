A sedimentary environment is a place where material is produced, moved or deposited. Steep mountain slopes release large angular blocks. Mountain streams can carry coarse sediment through bedrock canyons, and channels may split and rejoin as braided streams beyond the mountains. Fluvial describes river processes and deposits; it does not name one grain size.

On gentler terrain, a meandering stream is bordered by a floodplain. Floodwater spreads mud and sand across that surface. At a river mouth, the current enters standing lake or ocean water and slows. Sediment accumulates into a delta that builds outward. A floodplain lies alongside a channel; a delta grows at its mouth.

Wind transports sand and finer particles. Finer material can travel onward, leaving dunes composed mainly of well-sorted sand. A glacier instead carries a wide mixture, from ground rock flour to large blocks. Melting releases both water and sediment. Different agents can therefore produce quite different deposits from the same mountain source.

A lake contains several environments, from wave-worked shorelines to quieter central water. The shoreline may be sandy or rocky depending on the material available. In wetlands, saturated ground supports abundant vegetation. Water can limit exposure to oxygen, helping plant material become an important part of the accumulating sediment.

Topography, water and local geology control which environments develop. To interpret an ancient deposit, combine grain size, sorting, structures and biological remains. Angular blocks beside a cliff support a slope setting; rounded gravel in a channel supports stream transport. One sand layer alone cannot distinguish a desert, river or beach.
