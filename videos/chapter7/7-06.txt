Nonclastic sediment can form from dissolved components instead of fragments broken from older rock. As salty lake or sea water evaporates, minerals such as halite and gypsum can precipitate. Other chemical reactions also make minerals. Evaporation removes water; the dissolved material is left behind and becomes concentrated before precipitation.

Calcium-rich spring water can deposit layered calcite as travertine. Organisms also extract dissolved chemicals to build shells and skeletons. Accumulated carbonate remains can become limestone. Chemical and biological processes may work together, so the contrast concerns the stated formation pathway rather than an absolute separation of all natural examples.

Chalk is a soft, very fine-grained limestone made from microscopic calcium-carbonate remains. Dolostone can form when magnesium-bearing groundwater reacts with limestone, replacing calcite with dolomite. This changes the mineral composition of an existing carbonate rock; it is different from simply compacting an untouched pile of shell fragments.

Chert is silica rich. It can form from accumulated microscopic silica shells or from chemical precipitation, including nodules in carbonate rock. Iron formation contains thin layers rich in iron minerals, commonly with quartz, and much of it formed early in Earth history. Similar layering therefore does not mean identical composition or origin.

Wetlands can accumulate plant debris faster than it is completely destroyed. Peat retains much of the original plant texture and pore space. With burial, compaction and heating, peat loses water and oxygen and becomes coal. Carbon-rich coal records plant material; it is not an evaporite and is not made from a pile of quartz sand.
