Lithification turns loose sediment into rock. Rounded gravel produces conglomerate; angular gravel produces breccia. Sand-sized grains make sandstone. These names describe the preserved clasts, rather than a unique depositional setting. Grain composition and roundness refine the description after the main size-based classification.

Arkose contains at least twenty-five percent sand-sized feldspar in this course source. Preserved feldspar commonly supports rapid burial after derivation from crystalline rocks. Quartz sandstone contains mostly quartz, reflecting a quartz-rich source or destruction of weaker grains. Graywacke contains a mixture of mineral and rock-fragment compositions.

Siltstone contains silt-sized particles, commonly quartz. Shale contains very fine, aligned clay-rich material and splits into thin sheets or chips. That splitting behavior matters when distinguishing shale from other mudrocks. Use the source photographs with the texture descriptions, rather than choosing a rock from color alone.

Burial adds weight. During compaction, grains move closer together, pore space shrinks, and water or air is expelled. Platy clay particles can rotate toward parallel orientations as a layer thins. The grains remain solid: compaction is packing, not melting, and some pore space can remain even after burial.

Water moving through connected pores carries dissolved components. New minerals precipitate around grains and bind them as cement. Four common cements here are calcite, silica, clay minerals and iron oxides. Silica can make a resistant rock, calcite can redissolve, and iron oxides commonly redden it. Cementation and compaction are different contributions to lithification.
