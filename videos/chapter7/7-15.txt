The Front Range exposes old Precambrian granite and metamorphic basement. Sedimentary units rest above it and now dip eastward away from the mountains. Resistant Dakota sandstone forms a hogback, while weaker fine-grained layers erode into lower areas. Map colors identify units and ages; blue-colored old red rocks are not actually blue sandstone.

The lowest sedimentary package contains reddish coarse conglomerate, breccia and sandstone. Streams and debris flows carried sediment from the Ancestral Rockies onto older basement. Younger red units include coastal mudflat deposits and well-sorted dune sandstone. The source records changing environments rather than one continuous kind of red deposit.

The Jurassic Morrison Formation includes stream, wetland and lake deposits with dinosaur evidence. Above it, the Dakota Formation contains shoreline and beach features, including ripple marks, plant fossils and tracks. These units were deposited before their later tilting. A fossil track on a tilted bed records the old depositional surface, not a dinosaur walking up the modern slope.

Marine shale above the Dakota contains shells, shark teeth and fish bones. Sandstone and limestone interbeds record smaller shifts. Later sandstone, shale and coal mark a return to shoreline and land environments. The sea advanced across the region and was later overrun by deltas; compare the whole sequence to the paleogeographic maps.

The youngest Green Mountain deposits include crystalline and volcanic clasts derived from erosion of the rising Front Range. Deformation folded and faulted the buried sequence, and geological interpretation helped locate petroleum at depth. Separate deposition, later tilting, erosion and fluid trapping. Present elevation alone is not a complete history.
