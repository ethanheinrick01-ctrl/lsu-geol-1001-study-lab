Gravel includes granules, pebbles, cobbles and boulders. Sand is finer than gravel, while silt and clay together make mud. The supplied size table gives the exact boundaries. Clay is both a size term and a mineral-group name, so a clay-sized particle and a clay mineral describe different properties.

Angular clasts have sharp edges and corners. Repeated collisions and abrasion tend to remove those exposed corners, producing smaller, rounder pieces. Shape is distinct from size: a large cobble can be rounded, and a tiny sand grain can remain angular. Rounding does not by itself specify one transport distance.

Poorly sorted sediment mixes many sizes. Moderately sorted sediment has a more limited range, and well-sorted sediment concentrates similar sizes. Wind-built dunes commonly contain well-sorted sand because wind cannot supply gravel and finer particles may move onward. A mixture can be rounded and still poorly sorted.

Near a bedrock source, sediment commonly starts coarse, angular and poorly sorted. Transport can break it, round it and separate sizes. Farther along a stream or repeatedly worked by waves, finer and better-sorted material may remain. These are useful tendencies whose interpretation still depends on source rocks and available sediment.

The textbook identifies four controls: slope steepness, current strength, sediment supply and the transporting agent. Strong turbulent water can move larger clasts than weaker flow, but it cannot carry a size the source does not provide. Glaciers can move large mixtures, while wind is selective. Describe size, roundness and sorting before interpreting the setting.
