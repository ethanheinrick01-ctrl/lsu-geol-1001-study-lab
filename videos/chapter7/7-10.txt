Siltstone is dominated by silt. Mud includes both silt and clay, and its rock can be called mudstone or mudrock. Shale characteristically splits into thin chips or flakes. Fine grain size and splitting are separate observations. Interbedded sand or limestone and varying colors add information about a particular sequence.

Floodwaters spread fine sediment beyond a river channel, and slow water lets silt settle. Lakes receive mud from streams, wind and nearby slopes. Wind can also deposit extensive silt layers, especially from sediment ground by glaciers. Chemical weathering supplies clay-rich soils that may later become fine-grained rock.

Tidal mud flats are repeatedly flooded and exposed, sometimes leaving evaporite minerals after drying. Lagoons trap clay and mud delivered from land. Shallow continental seas and shelves also accumulate fines. Small grain size therefore supports quiet deposition, but does not by itself mean the water was very deep.

Fine particles carried by currents and wind can reach ocean basins. Organic-rich mud in relatively low-oxygen conditions can produce dark shale. The interpretation depends on composition and fossils as well as color. A dark fine-grained rock is evidence to investigate, not a direct measurement of an exact ancient water depth.

Shale, mudstone and siltstone commonly weather into soft slopes and loose chips. Debris from stronger cliffs can cover these weak units. Streams preferentially erode some weak layers, while intricately drained badlands develop in easily eroded sediment. Modern slope shape helps identify resistance; depositional environment still requires the rock evidence.
