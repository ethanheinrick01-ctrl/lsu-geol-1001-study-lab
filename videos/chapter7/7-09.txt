Sandstone is dominated by sand-sized grains, commonly quartz but sometimes feldspar, volcanic fragments or other minerals. It may contain some silt or gravel while remaining sandstone. Grains can be angular or rounded. Parallel layers, cross beds and massive-looking intervals record different patterns of deposition and preservation.

Desert dunes commonly produce well-sorted sand and large cross beds. Iron-oxide cement can give terrestrial sandstone a red color. Streams deposit sand in channels and during floods, commonly interlayered with finer floodplain sediment. Use structures and associated deposits together; neither red color nor sandstone alone proves desert dunes.

Beaches obtain sand from local rocks, reefs, rivers, wind and coastal currents. Shells or gravel may accompany it, although energetic waves can destroy fragile shells. Wind moves some beach sand into coastal dunes. Deltas also deliver sand through channels and shallow water, so several neighboring coastal settings can produce sandstone.

The source outcrop changes upward from marine shale with thin sand beds to thicker sandstone with less shale. A delta advanced over the earlier marine mud. This records a shift toward sandier, more landward conditions at that location. Read the whole vertical sequence rather than interpreting the upper sandstone in isolation.

Sand can remain on a continental shelf or move into deeper water. A turbidity current carries a slurry downslope and can leave a graded sand bed among quieter marine mud deposits. Sand near volcanoes may be rich in dark volcanic grains. Deep-water sandstone is therefore compatible with continental sediment delivered by a sudden current.
