Much groundwater occupies pores between sediment grains and fractures in rock. Connected openings allow water to move; an isolated opening can store water without transmitting it well. A sedimentary rock can therefore act as both a record of deposition and a host for a later fluid history.

Oil and natural gas form in organic-rich sedimentary rocks and can migrate upward. Some escape, while some collect where rock geometry and a seal trap them. Distinguish the source that generates petroleum from the reservoir that stores it and the trapping conditions that keep it underground. A porous bed alone does not prove an oil field.

Coal comes from buried plant material. Suitable limestone supplies material for manufactured cement. Salt can be mined from old deposits or harvested by evaporating brine. Migrating groundwater can concentrate uranium in sedimentary rocks. These resources have different origins even when all occur within a sedimentary sequence.

Modern sedimentary processes help interpret ancient deposits. Layers preserve changing environments, fossils, past climate and evidence of events such as landslides, storms and earthquakes. Reconstruct the event from its deposit and context. The record supports understanding of past change; it does not specify the exact date of a future hazard.

Sand and gravel are widely used construction resources. Material is excavated, screened into sizes and sometimes crushed. Selected sizes can become concrete aggregate, tile ingredients or fill beneath roads and buildings. Aggregate is the granular material; manufactured cement binds a concrete mixture. Natural cement between rock grains is another use of the same word.
