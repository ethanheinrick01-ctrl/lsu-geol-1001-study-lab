Physical weathering breaks rock apart without requiring a change in its minerals. Near-surface fracturing can accompany unloading or a fall from a cliff. Freezing water and growing salt crystals widen cracks. Roots can pry fractures apart, while burrowing exposes material. Thermal expansion can contribute, but this textbook explicitly qualifies its relative importance.

Chemical weathering changes or removes minerals. Dissolution puts soluble components into water. Oxidation forms oxides, including reddish iron oxides. Hydrolysis reacts water with silicate minerals and commonly produces clay plus dissolved material. Acids and other biological reactions can also break minerals down. These are chemical changes, not simply the rounding of a stone.

Starting composition matters. Soluble limestone can develop pits and grooves as water removes material. In granite, feldspar may alter to clay or survive as grains, resistant quartz commonly remains as sand, and dark minerals can yield clay and iron oxides. One original rock can therefore supply several sediment sizes and compositions.

Fracturing makes a rock weaker and increases the surface available to water, air and organisms. Physical breakup can accelerate chemical reactions, and chemical alteration can weaken rock for further breakup. Highly fractured parts of a unit may erode into notches or lower terrain. The two forms of weathering often reinforce each other.

After weathering loosens material, streams, waves, wind, ice and gravity can move it. Collisions break clasts, and abrasion scrapes surfaces and rounds exposed corners. Moving sediment can also scour bedrock and create more fragments. Keep the steps distinct: weathering produces loose or dissolved material, erosion removes it, and transport carries it elsewhere.
