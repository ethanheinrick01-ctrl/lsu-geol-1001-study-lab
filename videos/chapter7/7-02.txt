A coastline receives sediment from local erosion and from rivers, waves and currents. A beach can contain sand, shells or rounded gravel; not every coast has a sandy beach. Long narrow accumulations of sand can form barrier islands. Shallow lagoons or saltwater marshes commonly separate those islands from the mainland.

A lagoon is sheltered water behind a reef or island. Its nearshore sediment may come from land, while coral and shell debris can enter from a reef. In suitable shallow, warm, clear water, organisms build reefs from their hard parts. The reef reduces the wave energy reaching the protected shoreline.

Continental shelves and slopes accumulate mud, sand and carbonate material. When sediment becomes unstable, a dense mixture of water and sediment can move downslope as a turbidity current. Submarine canyons may funnel this material toward deeper water. Quiet offshore settling and sudden downslope transport can occur in the same basin.

Deep seafloor is cold and dark. Far from land, fine windblown dust and microscopic marine remains commonly dominate the sediment. Along a shoreline, tidal flats alternate between flooding and exposure. In hot dry settings, evaporation can leave salt and other minerals. Depth alone does not explain every deposit.

Wind can carry beach sand inland to coastal dunes and can return it toward the water when wind direction changes. Deltas extend offshore, where unstable fronts can deliver sediment to deeper water. Read the whole system: sediment supply, water clarity, organisms, wave protection and transport all help distinguish neighboring environments.
