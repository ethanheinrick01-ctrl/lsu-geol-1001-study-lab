A distinctive laterally traceable rock unit can be called a formation. A formation may contain many individual beds. Bedding can be defined by grain size, composition or color, and the boundary between rock units is a contact. Contacts may be sharp, gradational or scoured; a sharp boundary alone does not establish its cause.

Layers can record a discrete flood, changes in current strength or direction, a new sediment supply, changing sea level or changing climate. Each explanation needs the actual evidence. Different colors do not automatically mean different climates, and a thick bed does not supply a universal amount of elapsed time.

Shake mixed sediment in water and let it settle. Coarser particles settle first, with progressively finer material above. A normally graded bed therefore becomes finer upward as transport energy wanes or suspended sediment settles. Read the variation within one bed. Water left above a jar deposit is not another sedimentary layer.

A current moves grains over a dune or ripple crest. Grains accumulate on its down-current slope, and migrating dunes preserve inclined layers called cross beds. Cross beds can form in water as well as in wind. Large dune-scale sets and other clues support a desert interpretation; inclined layers alone do not prove one.

Parallel beds commonly form as layers accumulate on one another. A scoured contact instead records erosion before the next deposit. Keep parallel bedding, cross bedding and grading distinct: they concern layer orientation, inclined internal deposits and an upward grain-size change.
