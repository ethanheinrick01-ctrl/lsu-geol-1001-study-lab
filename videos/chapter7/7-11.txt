Limestone consists mainly of calcite and commonly contains fossils. It can dissolve and reacts with hydrochloric acid. Dolostone consists mainly of dolomite and generally resists dissolution better. Carbonate can be mixed with clay or quartz, so color alone does not distinguish the minerals or establish where the rock formed.

Limestone can form in evaporative lakes and as chemical deposits around springs. Porous layered travertine can accumulate in hot or cold springs, lakes and caves. Each new layer coats material already present. The lake pillars and spring deposits are carbonate examples without requiring a marine reef at the site.

Reef organisms extract calcium carbonate to construct shells and skeletons. Their remains and lime mud accumulate on shelves. Waves can break reef material into calcite sand that later becomes clastic limestone. Carbonate composition and clastic texture can therefore occur together. In the deepest sea, carbonate may dissolve instead of being preserved.

Magnesium-bearing fluids can interact with limestone and replace calcite with dolomite. Replacement may remain incomplete, leaving limestone and dolostone together. This is a chemical modification of the rock, distinct from moving sand grains or simply growing a new reef. Keep original sediment formation and later mineral replacement separate.

In dry settings, carbonate rocks can form resistant cliffs. In wetter conditions, dissolution exploits fractures and bedding, leaving openings, pillars and caves. Groundwater carries dissolved carbonate away. If a cave roof collapses, a sinkhole can form. Erosion resistance depends on climate and structure, so limestone does not always produce the same landform.
