A force is a push or pull. Stress is force divided by the area over which it acts, so the same force on a smaller area produces greater stress. Confining pressure acts equally from every direction; differential stress differs with direction.

Rock may translate to a new position, rotate to a new orientation, or change its shape and size. That internal change is strain. A tilted bed records rotation; flattened pebbles record internal strain. More than one response can occur together.

On the supplied strength graph, depth increases downward and strength increases to the right. In shallow brittle rock, confinement generally increases resistance to breaking. Deeper, hotter rock can deform by solid-state flow and strength decreases. The curve peaks near the transition.

The brittle–ductile transition is a change in dominant behavior, not a universal fixed depth or a melting line. Temperature, composition, fluids, pressure, and strain rate affect how a rock responds. The graph is a model, not a single depth that applies everywhere.
