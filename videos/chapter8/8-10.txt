Continental extension stretches and thins crust, commonly forming normal faults, tilted blocks, and grabens. Magma and warm fluids may accompany rifting. Extension and the pressure–temperature response vary from place to place during the evolution of a rift.

As continental rifting progresses to seafloor spreading, new oceanic crust forms at a ridge. Seawater can circulate through hot fractured rock and cause hydrothermal metamorphism. The hot-water pathway is especially important at mid-ocean ridges.

A strike-slip fault accommodates primarily horizontal relative motion. Looking across the fault, the opposite side moves right in a right-lateral fault and left in a left-lateral fault. Offset streams and roads preserve the displacement of originally continuous markers.

A bend or step in a strike-slip system can make adjacent rocks converge or pull apart. Local shortening can build folds and ridges, while local extension can produce a basin. The main lateral motion alone does not determine the sign of every bend.
