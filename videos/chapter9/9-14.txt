A young lava flow can overlie an older flow, establishing their order. Weathering and surface development add clues, while suitable isotope systems or buried charcoal can constrain eruption ages. Hazard evaluation needs the sequence and timing of activity. A dated past eruption alone does not schedule the next one.

A fault is younger than deposits it offsets and older than unaffected cover that seals its trace. Scarps also degrade through erosion. Along rivers, floodplains and raised terraces preserve different flooding histories. Soil, charcoal and surface ages can extend evidence beyond short instrumental records, with the meaning of each dated sample kept explicit.

Mineral exploration asks when an intrusion released fluids and how mineralized fractures relate to later rocks. Oil and gas exploration also needs event order: reservoir, folding, migration, trapping and possible later escape. The source anticline illustrates why a favorable rock sequence is only part of a resource history.

Sedimentary basins can store groundwater in connected pore spaces, while granite may carry water mainly through fractures. Fossils, relative relationships and dated volcanic beds help reconstruct the basin. Isotopes in groundwater can address when that water entered the system, a different question from when the enclosing rock formed.

An older wall fragment reused in younger masonry illustrates inclusions. Successive occupations can follow superposition, while tree rings and carbon-fourteen constrain suitable recent material. Much older human-ancestor sites may require dated volcanic layers and fossil correlation. Choose the method for the material and timescale, and preserve its position within the site.
