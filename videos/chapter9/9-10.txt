An impact transfers kinetic energy into target rock, causing shock, fragmentation and sometimes melting. Excavated fragments are thrown outward and form ejecta around a crater. Smaller events leave simple craters; larger ones can produce complex craters with central peaks. Shape reflects formation conditions, not a universal rule that one crater type is older.

Impacts were much more frequent when solar-system debris was abundant. Earth’s interior also retained formation heat and more radioactive heat production. Early volcanism included very hot ultramafic lava, and slightly metamorphosed lava survives as greenstone. The textbook treats the onset and style of early plate tectonics cautiously; present processes need not have operated at today’s rates.

Light gases from early accretion were readily lost. Volcanic outgassing supplied water vapor, carbon dioxide and other gases, while water eventually collected into oceans. The early atmosphere lacked significant free oxygen. Do not confuse oxygen bound inside minerals with oxygen gas available in air or dissolved in seawater.

Cyanobacteria released oxygen through photosynthesis. Dissolved iron could then oxidize and precipitate, helping form banded iron-rich deposits composed mainly of iron oxides and quartz. Oxygen production and atmospheric oxygen accumulation are separate steps because oxygen also reacts with surrounding materials. The rock record preserves evidence of these chemical changes.

The supplied textbook places the Great Oxygenation Event around two point three to two point four billion years ago. Oxygen stayed low through much of the Proterozoic, then rose again before the Cambrian. The cause and timing of the second transition remain research questions in the source.
