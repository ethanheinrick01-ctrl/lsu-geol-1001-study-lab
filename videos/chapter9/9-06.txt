Fossil assemblages change systematically upward through many rock sequences. This observed pattern is faunal succession. First establish which fossils occur in older and younger beds; then compare sites. Local environmental changes also affect which organisms are preserved, so one missing fossil does not automatically demonstrate a worldwide extinction.

Precambrian time makes up nearly ninety percent of Earth history and includes early simple life. The Paleozoic records diverse marine life and colonization of land. Dinosaurs characterize much of the Mesozoic, while mammals diversify prominently in the Cenozoic. These broad summaries do not mean each group first appeared exactly at its named boundary.

A population depends on food, water, habitat and interactions with other species. Climate change or loss of a required partner can alter survival. Reproductive strategy and adaptability affect how populations respond. Follow the changing habitat and population in this illustrative model; its numbers are not measured extinction data.

Competition can occur within a species or between species sharing scarce resources. Predator and prey populations also depend on one another. A specialist that relies on one food source is vulnerable when that source disappears. No organism changes because it decides to meet a need; population changes must be explained through biological processes.

Documented fossil changes are observations. Natural selection is an explanatory mechanism for evolutionary change. The textbook also discusses punctuated equilibrium, in which long intervals of relative stability alternate with comparatively rapid change. Rapid here means geological time, not an individual organism transforming instantly. Keep the evidence, proposed mechanism and tempo distinct.
