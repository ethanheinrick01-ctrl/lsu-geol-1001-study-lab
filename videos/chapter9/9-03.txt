An angular unconformity begins with layers deposited approximately horizontally. Later deformation tilts or folds them. Erosion then cuts across the tilted beds, and younger sediment buries the surface. Watch the old layers remain truncated beneath the new cover. The contact records several events and missing time, rather than one unusually thick bed.

A nonconformity forms when deep crystalline rock, such as granite, reaches the surface through uplift and erosion. Weathering can alter its exposed top. Younger sediment then covers the eroded granite and eventually becomes sedimentary rock. The critical relationship is sedimentary cover above eroded nonlayered crystalline basement.

A disconformity separates sedimentary packages whose beds remain parallel. Deposition stops, erosion or nondeposition removes part of the record, and sedimentation resumes. Because the packages can look similar, the gap may be difficult to recognize. Fossil age ranges can reveal missing time even when the contact is visually subtle.

The Grand Canyon and Ouray photographs show older steep or tilted rock beneath younger cover. Follow the actual boundary and compare bedding on both sides. Some examples involve metamorphic layering, so state the visible relationships rather than relying on one color. The textbook names and photographs provide the context for classification.

Every unconformity is younger than the rock it truncates and older than the deposits above it. Ages on those rocks bracket missing time; they do not recover every event removed by erosion. Name the type, explain the required sequence, and state what the preserved evidence cannot tell you about the missing interval.
