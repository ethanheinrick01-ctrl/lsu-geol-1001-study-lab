A fossil is preserved evidence of life: remains, an imprint or a trace. Hard shells, bones and teeth are more likely to survive than soft tissue. Rapid burial reduces scavenging, weathering and decay. The resulting record is selective; the abundance of durable fossils does not mean soft-bodied organisms were absent.

When a buried organism dissolves or decays, its shape can remain as an unfilled mold. If minerals later fill that cavity, the infill forms a cast. Keep the sequence straight: organism, burial, removal, cavity and possible filling. A mold and cast preserve related shapes through different material states.

Minerals can replace original material, as silica replaces wood. Compression can leave a carbon film, and quiet sediment can preserve an impression. Amber can entomb fragile structures. Stromatolites record layered constructions by microscopic organisms. These are different preservation routes; no single sequence describes every fossil in the chapter.

Footprints and trackways record movement across a surface. Burrows record animals disturbing soft sediment, even when their bodies are not preserved. Later sediment can fill these structures. Distinguish a body fossil from a trace fossil by asking whether you are seeing the organism itself or evidence of what it did.

Branching manganese-oxide dendrites can resemble plants, but they grow through inorganic mineral processes. Concretions grow within sediment and can also look biological. A concretion may contain a fossil without being a fossil itself. Classify the origin of the feature, using its structure and context rather than its suggestive outline alone.
