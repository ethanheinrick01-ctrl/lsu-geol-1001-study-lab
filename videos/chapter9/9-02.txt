First build the rock sequence. Sediment accumulates, depositional environments change, and the layers become rock. When uplift or retreating seas exposes the sequence, erosion begins removing material. A river can cut downward while tributaries and slope failure widen the canyon. The old rock and the young canyon record different events.

An erosion surface must postdate the rocks it truncates. If a lava flow later covers that surface, the surface must already exist before the eruption. Dating the rock below and the lava above places limits on the surface age. Neither number automatically dates the moment erosion began or ended.

A stream terrace preserves a former stream level above the modern channel. Incision left that older surface behind. Undisturbed surfaces also develop soil over time. Thick soil on an upland and little soil on recent stream sediment support a relative comparison in the supplied example. Climate and disturbance must still be considered.

Desert varnish accumulates iron and manganese oxides on exposed rock. Desert pavement develops as fine material leaves or moves downward and larger clasts concentrate near the surface. Dark varnish and a well-developed pavement can indicate long exposure, but abrasion or renewed deposition can interrupt or reset that record.

Cosmic rays can produce measurable isotopes in exposed stones. Laboratory analysis can constrain exposure duration. Combine that evidence with buried surfaces, terraces, soils and varnish rather than substituting bedrock age for landscape age. Ask what started the clock, what could disturb it, and whether the measured event is formation, exposure or burial.
