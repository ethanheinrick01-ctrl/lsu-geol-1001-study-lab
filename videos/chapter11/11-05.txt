Follow an oceanic plate toward a continent. The plate bends downward into a trench, which traps sediment. Material scraped from the descending plate contributes to an accretionary prism. Between the trench and the main mountain front lies the forearc basin. These features occupy specific positions: the trench is not the foreland basin, and the prism is not the volcanic arc.

Magma rises into the continent, crystallizing at depth or erupting at the surface. Compression can also fold and thrust the crust, making it thicker. Behind the main mountain belt, thrust sheets load the plate and bend it downward into a foreland basin. Sediment eroded from the rising mountains fills that depression. Some margins have weak compression and lack this complete thrust-and-foreland arrangement.

The overriding plate can shorten or extend. If the continent moves toward the slab relative to the asthenosphere, pushing against the slab promotes compression. If the overriding plate does not advance toward it, or moves away, slab retreat can stretch the upper plate. That is why extension can occur above a subduction zone even though the two plates converge overall.

Continental crust resists deep subduction because of its buoyancy. Collision underthrusts one continent beneath another, stacks crust, and raises mountains or a plateau. Thrust loads create adjacent basins. If the thickened crust becomes sufficiently hot and weak, it can spread sideways under its own weight, producing local normal faults. Collision effects can also extend far into the continental interior.

The Devonian Catskill system records an ancient collision and foreland basin east of today's continental interior. Thrust loading depressed the crust, while mountains supplied sediment westward. Coarser and thicker deposits near the eastern source preserve that relationship. Later uplift and erosion exposed the record. Use sediment distribution together with deformation to reconstruct the mountains and basin as one evolving system.
