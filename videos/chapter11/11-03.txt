Not every mountain requires an entire region of thickened crust. A local mountain can sit on the existing crust. Volcanic eruptions pile lava, ash, and scoria onto a surface, building anything from a small cinder cone to a large composite volcano. An eroded volcanic remnant may remain high because its rocks resist erosion, even after its original cone shape has disappeared.

Reverse faulting can raise one block over another. The Front Range and its tilted neighboring layers illustrate that relationship. Normal faults can also produce mountains, despite extending and thinning the region. One block drops into a basin while the neighboring block remains relatively high or rises. Near Death Valley, mountains and sediment-trapping basins are paired consequences of normal faulting.

Folding bends both rock layers and the land above them. A buried thrust can push layers upward without breaking through the surface. The Los Angeles example connects hidden structures with folded rock and damaged wells. A fold can also survive as relief long after deformation ends, if erosion strips away weak cover and leaves a resistant folded layer exposed.

Differential erosion creates mountains by removing surrounding rocks faster. Stone Mountain is granite that crystallized deep underground and was later uncovered. It is not a volcano simply because it is an igneous mountain. A hard cap can protect softer layers and leave a flat-topped mesa. Resistant tilted beds can produce ridges whose shapes follow the rock layers.

So ask what made the relief: material added at the surface, displacement on a fault, bending of layers, or unequal erosion? More than one process can contribute. A mountain's outline alone does not identify its origin. Use the rock types, layer geometry, fault relationships, and surrounding landscape to distinguish the mechanisms.
