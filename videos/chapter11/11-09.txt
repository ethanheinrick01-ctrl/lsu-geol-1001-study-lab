A tectonic terrane is a fault-bounded piece of crust with a geologic history different from its neighbors. Look for contrasts in rock sequences, ages, fossils, structures, chemistry, or metamorphism. The boundary is a major fault or shear zone. A terrane is a package of evidence and history, not simply any patch of ground with a different surface color.

Terranes can begin as oceanic crust, island arcs, continental fragments, oceanic islands, or plateaus. Oceanic pillow basalt and deep-marine sediment suggest a different setting from thick granitic continental crust. During convergence, a buoyant or thick piece can be scraped from a descending plate and attached to the continent. This addition is accretion, commonly accompanied by deformation and mountain building.

To date attachment, look for a relationship that crosses the boundary. An intrusion that cuts both terranes shows they were already together when it formed. A single sedimentary unit deposited across their boundary gives another younger limit. These dates constrain when joining had happened; they do not necessarily give the exact moment of collision.

Sediment containing fragments eroded from a neighboring terrane shows proximity by the time of deposition. Fossils can constrain former settings and connections, but an animal's ability to travel matters. Magnetic directions can reveal differences in original latitude or orientation. Similar ages alone do not prove two rocks formed together, especially when their environments and histories differ.

Alaska combines displaced continental pieces, oceanic material, arcs, and younger overlapping deposits. Wrangellia is interpreted to have traveled north before colliding with the western margin. Later strike-slip motion dispersed pieces along the coast. Matching sequences and ages connect those separated remnants. The sequence is formation elsewhere, transport, accretion, and possible later rearrangement; the present map preserves the result.
