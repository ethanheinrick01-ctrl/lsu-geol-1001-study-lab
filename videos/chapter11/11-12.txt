The Appalachians and Ouachitas lie inland from today's passive margins, but their folds and thrusts record ancient compression. Start with late Precambrian rifting. A continent separated from eastern North America, and spreading created an ocean. The continental edge then became a passive margin. The present coastline cannot tell you what kind of plate boundary occupied this region hundreds of millions of years ago.

Later, oceanic lithosphere attached to North America subducted beneath an offshore island arc. As the ocean narrowed, the arc approached and collided with the margin. Around four hundred fifty million years ago, that collision produced the Taconic mountain-building event. Orogeny means mountain building; each named event represents a distinct part of the history.

Around four hundred million years ago, Avalonia collided with eastern North America in the Acadian orogeny. Thrust sheets loaded and bent the crust, forming the foreland basin associated with the Catskill deposits. Distinguish this continental-fragment collision from the earlier island-arc collision. Both caused shortening, but the incoming crust and timing were different.

In the late Paleozoic, roughly three hundred thirty to three hundred million years ago, Gondwana collided with North America. The African and South American parts contributed to Appalachian and Ouachita mountain building. Large thrusts and folds stacked and shortened crust as Pangaea assembled. Erosion later carried mountain-derived sediment far away, leaving datable mineral grains that help trace its source.

By about two hundred twenty million years ago, extension began breaking the joined region apart. Fault-bounded basins accumulated red sediment and basalt. Jurassic spreading around one hundred eighty million years ago opened the Atlantic. The sequence is rifting, ocean opening, several collisions, then renewed rifting. Today's eroded mountains preserve the collision history even though the active boundary has moved offshore.
