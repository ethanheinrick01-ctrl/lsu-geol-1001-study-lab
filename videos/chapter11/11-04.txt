A basin is low relative to its surroundings, but basins do not all form the same way. Continental extension creates normal faults and downdropped blocks. Coarse debris collects near steep mountain fronts; finer sediment and sometimes evaporites accumulate in basin lakes. If rifting reaches seafloor spreading, the former rifted edge can cool and subside into a passive continental margin.

Compression also makes basins. Thrust sheets pile extra weight onto the crust, bending it down in front of the thrust belt. That is a foreland basin; the Persian Gulf is the example here. A forearc basin occupies a different position, between a volcanic arc and its trench. Similar sedimentary lowlands can therefore record very different tectonic settings.

Strike-slip movement can open a depression at a releasing bend or between appropriately arranged fault segments. This produces a pull-apart basin, rather than a basin at every bend. Broad regional subsidence can also result from cooling, movements at depth, or changes to denser minerals. These mechanisms operate over scales much larger than a single fault block.

The Cascade forearc lies between the volcanic arc and offshore subduction zone. Southern California contains basins linked to its complicated fault system. The Gulf Coast records rifting followed by passive-margin subsidence and thick sediment accumulation. Ancient basins may no longer be obvious depressions: their history survives in unusually thick sedimentary sequences.

Michigan's bedrock map forms a broad bull's-eye, with younger layers toward the center. In cross section, the layers thicken inward. That tells us the basin was subsiding while sediment accumulated.
