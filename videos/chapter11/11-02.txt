A regional mountain belt needs a regional explanation. At a continental subduction zone, magma adds crust while compression shortens it. Both can thicken the continent. In a continental collision, one buoyant continent is partly shoved beneath another, producing thrusts, folds, and deep crustal roots. Mantle upwelling provides another route: heating and replacement of dense lithosphere can lift a broad region.

The Andes illustrate subduction, magmatic additions, and shortening. The Himalaya and Tibetan Plateau record India's collision with Asia. The Alps also reflect continental collision, while western Canada combines shortening, magmatism, and accreted pieces. East Africa shows the importance of heating and mantle upwelling. Australia's eastern highlands are a useful caution: the supplied text leaves the age and cause of uplift under investigation.

Mountain building and erosion compete from the beginning. If uplift outpaces removal, relief can grow. When erosion removes mass, the buoyant crustal root rebounds upward. That brings formerly deep metamorphic and intrusive rocks toward the surface while erosion uncovers them. Rebound does not preserve mountain height forever. Continued removal gradually reduces both the mountain and its root.

Follow the removed material into neighboring basins. Deposited sediment adds weight, bends the crust downward, and creates room for further accumulation. The mountain and its sedimentary basin are therefore connected parts of one system. Erosion is not making rock disappear; it transfers material and changes the loading pattern.

Western North America's elevation reflects thickened crust and, in some places, thin warm lithosphere. Farther east, cooler thicker lithosphere helps explain lower elevations toward the Mississippi. The Appalachians have lost much of their former crustal thickness through erosion. The textbook's approximate rule is six kilometers of added crust for one kilometer of elevation, illustrated by Phoenix and Flagstaff. Treat it as a rule of thumb, not a universal conversion.
