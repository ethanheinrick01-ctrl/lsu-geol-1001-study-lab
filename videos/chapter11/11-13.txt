Western North America's history begins with late Precambrian rifting, broadly around six hundred million years ago. The identity of the departing landmass remains uncertain. By early Paleozoic time, the thinned western edge was a passive margin with a broad shallow shelf. Shallow-water deposits accumulated near the continent, with deeper-water sediment farther west. The coastline and the plate boundary were no longer the same thing.

During the middle and late Paleozoic, island arcs developed offshore and some collided with the margin. Thrusting moved deeper-water rocks eastward over shallower-water deposits and created a foreland basin. Meanwhile, the Ancestral Rockies farther inland are interpreted here as a response to collisions in the east, not simply the western arc collisions. Events happening at similar times need not share the nearest apparent cause.

Mesozoic subduction produced volcanic arcs and large intrusions, including the Sierra Nevada batholith. Terranes such as Wrangellia approached the continent. Compression built a long thrust belt; its load bent the continental interior downward. Combined with high sea level, that subsidence helped an interior seaway spread from the Gulf region toward the Arctic. Magmatism, shortening, loading, and sedimentation belonged to the same regional system.

Around eighty million years ago, the slab began descending at a shallower angle. In this model, it approached the base of the overriding lithosphere, shifted magmatism inland, and increased compression far from the coast. The Laramide orogeny formed uplifts and basins in the Rocky Mountain region from about eighty to forty million years ago. Changing the slab's geometry changed where the upper plate responded.

Later, convergence slowed and the stress pattern changed. Extension spread through much of the Southwest, building the Basin and Range and the Rio Grande Rift. Parts of the margin evolved into the San Andreas transform system as North America overran a spreading center. The textbook describes major regional stretching, but not uniform extension everywhere. Today's western landscape combines old collision structures, magmatic rocks, younger fault blocks, and active plate boundaries.
